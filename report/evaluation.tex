\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

\paragraph{Experiment Setup}. We ran our experiments on a physical
\texttt{Ubuntu-22.04} server with 8 Intel i5 cores and 16 GB RAM. We use 
\texttt{x86\_64-linux-gnu-g++} version \texttt{11.4.0} as our \texttt{C++} compiler.
We build the \textbf{SNAP} project in debug mode
\footnote{This means that assertions and debug outputs in the code are enabled.}
and with highest possible compiler optimization  level(\texttt{-O3}), and 
enabled link-time optimization(\texttt{-flto}).
For our parallel executions, we use $6$ worker threads when creating the thread
pool
\footnote{Along with the main(coordinator) thread, a total of 7 threads 
are used.}. For all of our experiments, we ensure that there is a gap of at
least 10 minutes between each execution to mimic the effect of rebooting
\footnote{Ideally, we should reboot before each execution. Since we use a
physical server, this posits much manual efforts.}.

\paragraph{Evaluation Metrics}. We expect our implementation to achieve 
performance gains via parallelized execution. Therefore, we compare the 
\textbf{execution time} and \textbf{CPU utility rate} of our 
manually-optimized code. Ideally, a thread pool with $N$ workers should yield
a CPU utility rate of $N\times 100\%$, and a speed-up of $N$\texttt{x}. However,
we did not try to evaluate the \texttt{scalability}, a desirable property of 
parallel algorithms, because of the limitation of computation capabilities of
 our physical server.

\subsection{Community-Affiliation Graph Model}

\par Community-Affiliation Graph Model\citep{yang2012structure,yang2012community} 
is a conceptual model of community network, capturing the overlapping nature of 
network communities.

\paragraph{Performance on Complete Graph} We measured the execution time of 
both original version of the \texttt{agmfitmain} program
\footnote{A frontend of AGM algorithm, see \url{https://github.com/Fudanyrd/snap/blob/master/examples/agmfit/agmfitmain.cpp}.}
 and our optimized version. For its input data, we constructed an undirected,
complete graph with $200$ vertices and $19900$ edges. This medium-size graph 
is large enough  for performance-benchmarking purposes.
% to demonstrate the difference between two implementations.
We ran each compiled program $8$ times.
The result is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:agmcomplete}. We also measured the CPU utility 
rate of both our optimized program  against original ones, the result is 
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:agmcpu}. We observe that our implementation make
good use of computation resources available, and completes the 
community detection task faster.

\subsection{Identifying Opinion Leaders}

\par To evaluate the effectiveness of our opinion leader identification approach, we visualize the network structure with node size and color encoded by different centrality measures. All visualizations are generated on the citation network, modeled as a directed graph with $1000$ nodes and $5403$ edges. By comparing structural patterns across different centrality-based visualizations, we assess whether the identified high-ranking nodes occupy prominent and meaningful positions in the network.

\paragraph{Degree-based Visualization}
Figure~\ref{fig:degreeviz} visualizes the network according to degree centrality. Nodes with high degree centrality tend to have many direct connections and are often located in locally dense areas of the network. While these nodes exhibit strong immediate influence due to their high connectivity, they do not always coincide with nodes that are globally influential or structurally central, demonstrating the limitation of degree centrality when used in isolation.

\paragraph{Facebook Betweenness-based Visualization}
In Figure~\ref{fig:facebookbetweennessviz}, node size and color reflect betweenness centrality in the Facebook network. High-betweenness nodes tend to appear at key junctions that connect otherwise loosely linked regions, acting as structural bridges across communities. These nodes are not necessarily the most densely clustered in the core; instead, they often lie on critical paths that enable information to travel between different parts of the network. This pattern suggests that betweenness centrality is effective for identifying broker-type opinion leaders in the Facebook graph.

\paragraph{Wiki-Vote PageRank-based Visualization}
In Figure~\ref{fig:wikivotepagerankviz}, node size and color reflect PageRank scores in the Wiki-Vote network. High-PageRank nodes are typically located in the dense core of the directed graph, where they receive strong endorsement-like links from other influential nodes. Compared with betweenness, PageRank emphasizes global importance driven by incoming connectivity and recursive influence, making it suitable for highlighting authoritative opinion leaders who attract attention from many prominent actors in the voting network.

\par Overall, the visualization results demonstrate that different centrality measures capture distinct structural roles of influential nodes, while the composite score effectively integrates these roles. This confirms that our centrality-based approach can successfully identify opinion leaders with diverse yet meaningful structural characteristics.
